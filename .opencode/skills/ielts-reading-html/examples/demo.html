Reading Passage 1

The Return of the Nightingale

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 1–10, which are based on Reading Passage 1 below.

AEvery spring, the nightingale's song returns to the woods and gardens of southern England. For centuries the bird has been celebrated in poetry for the richness of its voice, yet its habits remain surprisingly poorly understood. Nightingales are shy, brown birds that spend most of the year hidden in dense undergrowth, and they are far more often heard than seen. Even experienced birdwatchers may wait a whole morning for a glimpse of one.

BIn the 1950s, amateur recordists began noticing that nightingales in busy urban parks sang differently from those in quiet countryside. The urban birds produced songs with higher minimum frequencies and sang at night more often. At first these differences were dismissed as quirks of individual birds. Later studies, however, showed that the pattern was consistent across cities and across years.

CThe most obvious explanation is traffic noise. Low-frequency engine rumble can mask the lower notes of a nightingale's song, forcing the bird to shift its performance upwards. A study led by Dr Elena Marsh compared 12 populations in and around London. Birds living within 200 metres of a major road sang, on average, 6 decibels louder than birds in quieter sites. The shift was greatest at dawn, when traffic levels rise sharply.

DYet noise may not be the whole story. Nightingales also sing at night in cities, when traffic is lighter, so the need to compete with engines cannot explain everything. Marsh's team suggests that artificial light may play a role. Street lamps can make birds active for longer, and a longer singing period may allow them to attract mates while avoiding the hottest part of the day. The researchers are careful to say that this idea remains speculative.

EWhatever the cause, the changes are not necessarily harmful. A louder, higher song may still carry the information that females need: how healthy a male is and how much energy he can invest in display. The real concern is that urban nightingale numbers have fallen by almost a third since 1990. Protecting the scrubby edges where the birds nest is likely to matter more than reducing the volume of their song.
